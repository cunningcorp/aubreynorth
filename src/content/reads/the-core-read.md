A man stands alone at the end of his lawn, at night, reaching toward a green light on the far side of the bay.

He owns the mansion behind him. He throws parties for hundreds of people he doesn't know, week after week, on the theory that one night the right person might wander in. None of it is the point. The point is the light across the water — the dock of the woman he lost five years ago.

That image, from the opening of *The Great Gatsby*, is a hundred years old and has not aged a day. And it answers the question directly: what makes a character compelling is not charm, not backstory, not being likable. It is character plus motive — a person and the thing they want but never quite get.

We call that the Core.

## The want is the engine

Strip Gatsby of the want and look at what's left. A rich man with a large house and good parties. Nobody would read past the second chapter.

The mansion, the shirts, the reinvented name — every detail becomes interesting only because it is *for* something. Each one is an instrument of the want. The audience isn't investing in the man; it is investing in the reach.

Writers are told to make characters "relatable," to give them flaws, to write a detailed backstory. All of it is set dressing without a motive underneath. An audience can follow a liar, a criminal, even a bore — what it cannot follow is someone who wants nothing. A character with everything and no want is scenery.

The want does one more job, quietly: it tells the audience what to watch for. Once you know what Gatsby is reaching for, every scene declares itself. The parties are not parties — they are waiting rooms. The audience reads each moment against the want, without being told to, and that is what "invested" actually means. Not liking a character. Knowing what they're reaching for, and needing to see how close they get.

## Never quite gets the thing

The second half of the definition carries the weight: the thing they want but never quite get.

If the want is ever fully satisfied, the story is over; there is nothing left to watch. The green light works because it stays across the water.

So the Core is not a goal to be achieved by the final act. It is all the tension that needs to be sustained.

## The business version

Most businesses, when asked what makes them compelling, answer the way bad character sheets do: traits. Established 2011. Twelve capabilities. Four values. Friendly, professional, passionate…

Traits are scenery. What's missing is the want — what the business is reaching for, on behalf of the customer. The want that isn't quite finished yet.

A firm that exists to close a specific gap in its customer's world has a Core; a firm that lists its qualities is just a nice brochure. One earns investment the way Gatsby's lawn does. The other is a rich man with a large house.

And the same second half applies. The want has to stay live. "We became the market leader" is a want that closed — a story with nothing left to watch. The businesses people stay invested in are the ones still visibly reaching, where the customer can read every move against the thing that is being reached for.

Finding that reach — the one thing the story is actually about — is the same edit we described in [the Narrative Read](/reads/the-narrative-read/): deciding what leads and what gets left on the floor. The Core is what the edit points at.

No core, no reason for the idea to exist. That is as true of a company as it is of a character on a dock, looking across the water at a light that never comes closer.
